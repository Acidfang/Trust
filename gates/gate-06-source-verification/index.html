    Gate 6: Source Verification

    Gate 6: Source Verification

    What Is This Gate?

    Source Verification is the ability to evaluate where information comes from and whether it’s trustworthy. It prevents both credulity (believing everything) and cynicism (believing nothing).

    A person with Source Verification distinguishes:

    • Hearsay (“My friend told me…”)
    • Authority (“The expert says…”)
    • Evidence (“Research shows…”)
    • Direct experience (“I tested it myself…”)

    The Core Distinction

    • With Source Verification: “I heard this from [source]. The source is [reliable/unreliable] because [evidence]. I’ll treat the information as [strong/weak].”
    • Without Source Verification: “I heard this and it sounds right” OR “I heard this and it’s probably wrong.”

    Why It Matters

    Source Verification protects against both gullibility and paranoia. It’s the gate between consuming information uncritically and becoming stuck in skepticism-paralysis.

    How Gate 6 Appears

    In Children (Ages 8-12)

    The child with Source Verification:

    • Hears a claim: “X is true”
    • Asks: “How do you know that?”
    • Evaluates answer: “Did you test it? Did an expert say it? Where did they learn it?”
    • Makes judgment: “I’ll believe it if…” or “I’ll test it myself”
    • Continues learning without gullibility

    The child without Source Verification:

    • Hears a claim: “X is true”
    • Takes it as true if it comes from “authority” (anyone who sounds sure)
    • Accepts it without evaluation
    • Becomes believer in whatever adult/peer says
    • Alternately, becomes cynic (“Nothing is true; everyone lies”)

    Physical Signs:

    • Asks “How do you know?” questions
    • Tests claims independently
    • Changes mind when evidence shows different
    • Curious about how people know things

    In Teens (Ages 13-18)

    The teen with Source Verification:

    • Sees social media claim
    • Notices: “This is from a stranger’s account”
    • Asks: “Why would they post this? What’s their incentive?”
    • Looks for corroboration: “Do other reliable sources say this?”
    • Forms opinion based on source quality, not just frequency

    The teen without Source Verification:

    • Sees social media claim
    • Takes it as true because “lots of people share it”
    • OR rejects all social media as false
    • Doesn’t distinguish between careful sources and careless ones
    • Becomes either credulous or paranoid

    Behavioral Signs:

    • Can articulate why they believe/don’t believe something
    • Cites sources when sharing information
    • Changes mind when presented with better sources
    • Distinguishes between “I saw this” and “This is verified”

    In Adults (Ages 25+)

    The adult with Source Verification:

    • Hears news claim
    • Checks: “Who reported this? What’s their track record?”
    • Looks for: “Do multiple independent sources confirm this?”
    • Evaluates: “What incentive does each source have?”
    • Forms opinion based on quality of sources, not on wanting to believe

    The adult without Source Verification:

    • Hears claim
    • Believes it if it fits their worldview
    • OR rejects it if it doesn’t, regardless of evidence
    • Becomes tribal (believes their “side,” rejects other “side”)
    • Increasingly isolated in filter bubble

    Behavioral Signs:

    • Can change mind when shown better sources
    • Reads across different viewpoints
    • Can explain why they trust/don’t trust sources
    • Acknowledges uncertainty (“I don’t know enough to judge”)

    Common Blockers (Where People Get Stuck)

    Blocker 1: Authority Substitution

    The Pattern: Person trusts [authority figure] and takes all claims they make as true.

    “The doctor said X, so X must be true” (without examining doctor’s source or incentive).

    The Result: Gullibility; can be manipulated by anyone with perceived authority.

    The Cost: Vulnerable to exploitation; stops thinking independently.

    Breaking It: “Even experts should explain their sources. Let’s look at where this expert learned this.”

    Blocker 2: Frequency = Truth

    The Pattern: Person believes something is true because they hear it repeatedly.

    “Everyone says this, so it must be true” (confusing consensus with evidence).

    The Result: Vulnerable to misinformation that spreads widely.

    The Cost: Believes false things that are popular; misses true things that are unpopular.

    Breaking It: “Who originally said this? Do we know if they had evidence?”

    Blocker 3: Tribal Loyalty

    The Pattern: Person believes sources from their “side” and rejects sources from other “side.”

    “My side has the truth; the other side are liars.”

    The Result: Can’t learn from people outside tribe; becomes isolated.

    The Cost: Polarization; inability to find common ground; stuck in echo chamber.

    Breaking It: “This source is from the ‘other side,’ but what if they happened to be right about this specific thing?”

    Blocker 4: Cynicism Collapse

    The Pattern: Person has been wrong about sources before → concludes all sources are untrustworthy.

    “Everyone lies; nobody can be trusted.”

    The Result: Paralysis; stops learning; becomes conspiracy theorist.

    The Cost: Isolation; can’t cooperate; stuck in defensive skepticism.

    Breaking It: “Some sources are more trustworthy than others. Let’s identify which ones.”

    Supported Paths (How to Help Someone Through)

    For Parents

    Starting Point: Assess source awareness

    • Do they question where information comes from?
    • Can they distinguish good sources from poor ones?
    • Do they test claims or just believe/reject?

    Path 1: Source Questions

    1. When child hears claim, ask: “Where did that come from?”
    2. “How does that person know that?”
    3. “Have you tested it yourself?”
    4. Normalize questioning sources

    Path 2: Source Comparison

    1. Show different sources for same topic
    2. “This source says X; this source says Y”
    3. “Why do you think they differ?”
    4. “Which source seems more trustworthy? Why?”

    Path 3: Direct Testing

    1. For testable claims: “Let’s test it ourselves”
    2. “What we tested showed [result]”
    3. “Now we know from direct experience, not just belief”
    4. Direct verification is most powerful

    For Educators

    Starting Point: Assess information literacy

    • Can students distinguish sources?
    • Do they cite sources for claims?
    • Can they evaluate source credibility?

    Path 1: Source Analysis

    1. Teach framework: “How to evaluate sources”
      • Author expertise
      • Publication venue
      • Evidence presented
      • Incentives/biases
      • Date/currency
    2. Practice analyzing same topic across multiple sources
    3. Students learn to distinguish reliable from unreliable

    Path 2: Fact-Checking Practice

    1. Present claims that sound plausible but are false
    2. Have students research source
    3. “Did this claim come from reliable source?”
    4. “What does reliable source actually say?”

    Path 3: Incentive Awareness

    1. Help students notice: “What does this source have to gain?”
    2. “Is the source trying to inform or persuade?”
    3. “What bias might the source have?”
    4. Students learn incentives shape information

    For Managers

    Starting Point: Assess organizational information culture

    • Do people verify sources or spread rumors?
    • Can they distinguish reliable from unreliable internal information?
    • Do they cite sources?

    Path 1: Transparent Sourcing

    1. In meetings, when claim is made, ask: “Where did this come from?”
    2. “Who verified this?”
    3. “What’s the evidence?”
    4. Model source-questioning behavior

    Path 2: Information Standards

    1. Establish: “Claims should be sourced”
    2. Distinguish: “Proposal (idea)” vs. “Data (verified fact)”
    3. Require team to differentiate
    4. Culture shifts toward verification

    Path 3: Multi-Source Leadership

    1. Leader reads across multiple perspectives
    2. Synthesizes: “Here’s what I learned from [source], [source], [source]”
    3. Doesn’t dismiss sources because they’re “other side”
    4. Team learns sophisticated source handling

    Integration with Other Gates

    Gates 5-6 Sequence

    • Gate 5 (Consequence Management): You learn from real outcomes
    • Gate 6 (Source Verification): You learn which sources predict real outcomes

    Together: you can navigate information strategically.

    Gate 6 Enables Gate 7

    If you verify sources well, you build accurate models of reality.

    • Reliable sources give you reliable information
    • You can form accurate sense of causality over time (Temporal Continuity)

    Without Source Verification: you base your sense of reality on unreliable sources.

    Gate 6 Blocks Without Care

    If blocked:

    • Person is credulous (believes everything, exploited easily)
    • OR paranoid (believes nothing, isolated)
    • Either way, can’t navigate information-rich environment

    Quick Diagnostic

    Does this person have Source Verification (Gate 6)?

    Ask these questions:

    1. Source Questioning: When hearing claim, do they ask where it comes from?
      • “That’s an interesting claim; where did you learn that?” → Has Source Verification
      • “Okay, I’ll believe that” → Lacks it
    2. Credibility Assessment: Can they evaluate trustworthiness of source?
      • “This source has expertise/incentive/track record…” → Has it
      • “I don’t know if it’s true; I just heard it” → Lacks it
    3. Cross-Checking: Do they verify across multiple sources?
      • “Let me check if other sources confirm this” → Has it
      • “I read it once; must be true” → Lacks it
    4. Source Diversity: Can they learn from sources outside their preference?
      • “This is from the other side, but they make a good point about X” → Has it
      • “Everything from that side is wrong” → Lacks it

    If most answers show lack of Source Verification: Gate 6 is blocked. Start by teaching them to ask “How do you know?” about claims and to check sources before believing.
